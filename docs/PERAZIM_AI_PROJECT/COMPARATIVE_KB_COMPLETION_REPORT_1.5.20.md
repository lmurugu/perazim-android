## 2. Entry-by-entry findings

### Indian religions, rebirth and pluralism

**`karma` — approved.** The entry separates Hindu, Buddhist and Jain accounts instead of treating them as one doctrine. Hindu material is supported by the Bhagavad Gita (Telang translation, 1882) and academic treatments of Madhva and Ramanuja. Buddhist intention and karmic causation are supported by Anguttara Nikaya 6.63 (Bhikkhu Sujato) and Mark Siderits's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on the Buddha. The Jain karma-as-matter description is now carefully attributed to Jain philosophical commentary and specialist scholarship, including Marie-Hélène Gorisse's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry and Ana Bajželj's peer-reviewed 2026 *Journal of Indian Philosophy* article, which analyses Pūjyapāda's and Akalaṅka's commentaries on the Tattvārtha-sūtra. The entry expressly distinguishes biblical sowing-and-reaping from rebirth-based karma; Galatians 6:7–8 is not presented as reincarnation.

**`reincarnation` — approved.** The entry distinguishes Hindu and Jain transmigration of a continuing self from Buddhist rebirth understood through causal continuity without an eternal self. Sources include Bhagavad Gita 2.20 and 2.22 (Telang), Samyutta Nikaya 22.59 (Sujato), the Acaranga Sutra (Jacobi), Gorisse's Jaina philosophy study and Siderits's Buddha entry. It notes that some Buddhist thinkers have rejected rebirth rather than treating all Buddhist schools as identical.

**`nirvana_moksha` — approved.** Buddhist nirvana, Hindu moksha and Jain liberation are separately explained. The entry identifies Advaita Vedanta (Śaṅkara), Viśiṣṭādvaita (Rāmānuja) and Dvaita (Madhva) as distinct Hindu schools, rather than defining Hindu teaching through one school. Evidence includes Udana 8.3 and Anguttara Nikaya 6.63 (Sujato), Siderits and Gorisse (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), and Neil Dalal, Shyam Ranganathan and Valerie Stoker's academic treatments of Śaṅkara, Rāmānuja and Madhva.

**`all_paths` — approved.** Religious pluralism is presented as a philosophical response to religious diversity, with John Hick's influential form attributed through David Basinger's full Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry. It is not attributed to Hinduism, Buddhism or New Age as a whole. The Christian evaluation uses John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 without denying that people in other traditions may recognise particular truths or moral goods.

**`divine_self` — approved.** The entry identifies the specific Vedanta schools and avoids the caricature that all Hindu traditions teach “everyone is God.” Its summaries of Advaita, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita are grounded in the named academic treatments by Dalal, Ranganathan and Stoker. The primary commentaries of all three teachers were not directly read; the entry attributes the descriptions to the named scholarship rather than pretending those primary commentaries were checked.

### Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Reformed doctrine

**`purgatory` — approved.** The Catholic description is supported by Catechism paragraphs 1030–1032, the Council of Florence's *Laetentur caeli* (6 July 1439), and the Council of Trent's Session 25 (1563). The Vatican's Latin Florence decree directly states that souls of the truly penitent who die before completing satisfaction are purified after death and that the suffrages of the living help them. The Catechism's paragraph 1032 appeals to Judas Maccabeus and prayer for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12:43–46. The Orthodox comparison is attributed to the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America, which explicitly says the Orthodox Church does not believe in purgatory while Orthodox churches pray for the departed. The entry does not equate prayer for the departed with the Catholic doctrine of purgatory. The Westminster Confession, chapter 32.1, represents the Reformed rejection of a third state of purification.

**`saint_prayer` — approved.** Catholic teaching is represented by Catechism paragraph 2683. Orthodox practice is supported by official Orthodox Church in America resources: “Canonization,” “Prayer,” and “Intercessory Prayer,” which explain asking saints to pray for believers. The Reformed response is represented by Westminster Confession 21.2. The Scripture evaluation reads 1 Timothy 2:5 within 1 Timothy 2:1–7: it affirms Christ's unique mediation without falsely claiming that Christians may not pray for one another. An unsourced claim about the exact historical origin of the practice was removed.

**`mary_mediator` — approved.** Catholic doctrine is described using Catechism paragraphs 963–971 and the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith's *Mater Populi Fidelis* (4 November 2025). The relevant paragraphs 22, 24–27 and 53 were checked: the note says the title *Co-redemptrix* is inappropriate for defining Mary's cooperation, requires special prudence for *Mediatrix*, and insists that any Marian mediation is subordinate and analogous to Christ's unique mediation. Pius XII's *Munificentissimus Deus* (1950) is the source for the Catholic definition of the Assumption. Orthodox material checked affirms Mary as Theotokos; it is not used to attribute the Catholic titles *Mediatrix* or *Co-redemptrix* to Eastern Orthodoxy. The Reformed evaluation distinguishes honouring Mary as the mother of the Lord from assigning a creature a share in Christ's unique saving mediation.

**`icons_veneration` — approved; previous hold closed.** The entry is supported by the Second Council of Nicaea's 787 decree (Percival English translation, 1900), Catechism paragraphs 2129–2132, and the Orthodox Church in America's “Iconoclasm” account. These sources distinguish the honourable reverence paid to an image from the worship due to God alone. The Reformed objection is represented by Westminster Confession 21.1 and Westminster Larger Catechism Q109. The entry does not claim that Scripture prohibits all images: it notes the cherubim commanded for the mercy seat (Exodus 25:18–22) and focuses the objection on religious bowing, service and worship. The historical English translation is old; no Greek term is quoted from its corrupted transliteration.

**`soul_sleep` — approved.** The entry represents the unconscious-death position using Seventh-day Adventist Fundamental Belief 26 and the Christadelphian statement of belief. It represents a conscious intermediate state through Westminster Confession 32.1 and Catechism paragraph 1022. Its own biblical evaluation reads Ecclesiastes 9 in context and considers Ecclesiastes 12:7, Daniel 12:2, Philippians 1:23 and 2 Corinthians 5:6–8. Luke 16:19–31 is identified as a parable, and Luke 23:43 is not treated as an uncontested proof on its own. The entry avoids claiming that every Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant and Evangelical tradition formulates the intermediate state identically.

**`conditional_immortality` — approved as comparative content only.** The revised definition distinguishes conditional immortality (immortality is not an innate human possession but God's gift) from annihilationism (a particular account of the wicked's final fate). It describes the specific positions of Jehovah's Witnesses, Seventh-day Adventists and many Christadelphian congregations, while representing the traditional eternal-punishment view through the Westminster Confession 33.2 and Catechism paragraph 1035. Revelation 20:10 is not used by itself to settle the fate of all human beings. **The entry explicitly leaves Perazim's position unset.**

**`canon_books` — approved.** The entry retains the authorised 66-book Protestant canon for the Perazim app. It describes the Catholic list through Council of Trent Session 4 (1546), the Reformed position through Westminster Confession chapter 1, and the Orthodox “longer canon” through the Orthodox Church in America's official “Canon of Scripture” answer. Metropolitan Macarius Bulgakov's 19th-century *Introduction to Orthodox Theology* provides additional historical Orthodox context. The entry does not treat one OCA answer or one historical Orthodox handbook as a binding universal list for every Orthodox jurisdiction. Deuteronomy 4:2 and Revelation 22:18–19 are not presented as direct proof of the exact number of canonical books.

**`group_question` — approved.** The policy permits the Guide to name and describe Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and other religious groups from their own sources, distinguish institutional doctrine from local practice and individual adherent belief, and evaluate doctrine against Scripture. Catholic Nicene teaching is supported by the Vatican Catechism and Creed; Orthodox Nicene and Christological claims are supported by the Orthodox Church in America's Creed and Incarnation pages and a second Orthodox jurisdiction's liturgical creed page. The policy does not give verdicts on individual members' eternal destiny.

### Denominations and disputed Christian doctrines

**`works_salvation` — approved.** Jehovah's Witness and Latter-day Saint teachings are described separately. The official JW salvation explanation says works demonstrate living faith and do not earn salvation; it also describes the need to continue exercising faith. The official LDS “Salvation” page distinguishes salvation from physical death, salvation from sin, and eternal life/exaltation. The official “Becoming Like God” essay describes exaltation through Christ's Atonement and obedience to gospel laws and ordinances. The entry does not collapse these distinct teachings into a generic claim that either group simply teaches “salvation by works.” The Christian evaluation distinguishes works as fruit of saving faith (Ephesians 2:8–10; James 2:17–18) from works as a ground or contribution to justification.

**`trinity_denial` — approved.** Three non-Nicene positions are distinguished rather than conflated: UPCI Oneness teaching (2023 Articles of Faith), Jehovah's Witness teaching, and Latter-day Saint teaching on the Godhead. The Oneness description is now supported by the UPCI's own 2023 Articles of Faith. The JW description uses official JW sources; the LDS description uses the official Godhead page. The Scripture evaluation was corrected so John 14:26 is used for the Father's sending of the Spirit, while John 3:16–17 and Galatians 4:4 support the Father's sending of the Son. It avoids applying “modalism” as an unqualified self-description of Oneness adherents.

**`jesus_created` — approved.** The created-being claim and the identification with Michael have separate official JW sources. The Bible Teach page “Who Is Jesus Christ?” paragraph 11 says Jesus was God's first creation and the only one directly created by God; the separate Michael page supports the JW identification of Jesus with Michael. The biblical evaluation uses complete KJV wording in John 1:1 and 1:3, Colossians 1:15–16 and Hebrews 1:5–6, and accurately notes that Jude 9 does not itself say Michael is not Jesus.

**`saturday_sabbath` — approved.** The official General Conference of Seventh-day Adventists 28 Fundamental Beliefs support the Trinity (FB2), remnant (FB13), Sabbath (FB20), unconscious death (FB26) and final destruction (FB27) descriptions. The official Adventist Biblical Research Institute's “Answers to Questions on the Mark of the Beast and End Time Events” explains the Adventist interpretation of a future Sunday-enforcement final test. The Guide does not say that present-day Sunday worshippers are already condemned or bear the mark of the beast.

**`christian_science` — approved.** Christian Science's official tenets and FAQ are used to describe its own claims. The entry correctly states that it affirms the crucifixion and resurrection in its own doctrinal terms while interpreting them through its teaching on Spirit, Soul and the nothingness of matter, which differs from the historic Christian confession of Christ's bodily resurrection. It distinguishes Jesus from the Christ in Christian Science's own account and does not claim the movement uniformly forbids medical care; its FAQ says adherents may choose their healthcare.

### New Age, secular worldviews and Islam

**`new_age_energy` — approved.** The movement is described as diverse using Margrethe Løøv's 2024 Cambridge University Press study, which characterises New Age as a mosaic of spiritual currents without one common founder, leader, institution, dogma or scripture. The energy-field claim is attributed specifically to Barbara Ann Brennan's *Hands of Light*, not to all New Age teaching. The publisher identifies the book within the Bantam New Age Books line and describes the human-energy-field claim; Brennan's author-founded school describes the same teaching. Those health claims are explicitly presented as metaphysical claims, not established scientific facts.

**`secular_worldview` — approved.** The entry distinguishes philosophical atheism, psychological and epistemological agnosticism, ontological naturalism, methodological naturalism and secular humanism. Sources include Paul Draper's and David Papineau's Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries and Humanist Manifesto III. It does not conflate atheism with naturalism, scientific method with metaphysical naturalism, or secular humanism with moral relativism.

**`islam_no_crucifixion` — approved.** The entry paraphrases Qur'an 4:157–158 and describes a common classical Muslim reading that denies Jesus was crucified. Al-Tabari and Ibn Kathir commentaries support the report of substitution readings, but the model text does not claim that every Muslim accepts one identical account. Louay Fatoohi's 2023 article and Ian Mevorach's 2017 article are used with their access limits accurately stated; only abstracts/endnotes were read, not their full articles. The Christian response gives the New Testament's crucifixion and resurrection testimony.

**`islam_son_denial` — approved.** The entry uses Qur'an 4:171, 5:72–75 and 112:1–4 as primary texts, paraphrased rather than quoted. It explains that Islam honours Jesus as Messiah and prophet but rejects divine Sonship. It expressly warns that the Qur'anic polemic against “three” must not be treated as an accurate description of Nicene Christianity, which confesses one God in Trinity rather than three gods or a physical sonship. Tafsir material was only partly read, so detailed universal claims about Muslim commentary are not made.

### African traditions

**`ancestors` — approved with explicit evidence limitations.** The entry states that African traditional religions are diverse and avoids a single pan-African doctrine. Mbiti's “living-dead” terminology is attributed through academic secondary sources because the original book was lending-restricted. The Kikuyu example is limited to Jomo Kenyatta's accessible article abstract. The Akan example is attributed to Anthony Ephirim-Donkor's peer-reviewed monograph record and abstract, which describes the ancestors' stool and propitiatory rites; the full book was not read. The wording does not generalise these examples to all Africans, to Embu, or to every Kikuyu or Luo community. Cultural remembrance is distinguished from invoking the departed, seeking their guidance or offering religious rites.

## 3. Source-access and confidence policy applied

The entries do not pretend that inaccessible primary sources were read. Instead:
- Official primary or institutional sources are used when available.
- Scholarly secondary evidence is explicitly labelled as such when a primary source could not be accessed.
- Claims are narrowed to the source's actual scope.
- Unread sections and partial abstracts are not cited as though the whole document had been read.
- Source limitations are retained in the internal provenance notes, which are not sent to the model.

The following limitations remain visible in the source register: Mbiti's primary book is inaccessible; the Kenyatta paper is accessible only as an abstract; the Ephirim-Donkor full book was not read; some Islamic tafsir/commentary was only partly read; several Hindu Vedanta descriptions rely on specialist academic summaries rather than directly read primary commentaries; and the 2025 Vatican Marian note is relied on only at the specific paragraphs checked. These limitations do not invalidate the narrower claims now made in the model-facing text.
