**Two things the data does not show:**
- Nothing in the reports above shows *why* people delay or avoid marriage. Explanations come from interviews and opinion pieces (Section B).
- No Kenyan survey reviewed here measures men's and women's attitudes to marriage side by side. The US figure below cannot stand in for it.

### A2. Attitudes to marriage (mostly outside Kenya)

- **US, Monitoring the Future, 2023 (Pew, 14 Nov 2025):** 74% of 12th-grade boys and 61% of 12th-grade girls say they want to get married someday. In 1993 the figures were 76% of boys and 83% of girls. The girls' share fell 22 points; the boys' share barely moved. Pew gives no cause for the gap. **Not Kenyan data.** Use only to show that the gap is recorded elsewhere.
- **Mwelekeo Insights, February 2025 (cited by The Star, 9 Jul 2025):** 29% of Nairobi women consider marriage a key life goal, and 53% of 2,687 interviewed women say they do not aspire to marriage. Sample and method not published in the article. Treat as **unverified** until the report is found.

### A3. Economic and structural figures (second-hand, dated)

- *The Nation* (undated page, read 25 Nov 2026) cites a 2016 Consumer Insight study: 52% of Kenyan women would choose a better-paying job over marriage. Also a 2016 "#SEXMONEYFUN" study: 35% of respondents admitted to having a "sponsor" (a richer partner for money). **Both are old, commercial, and not checked against the original.** Do not use as statistics in an answer. They can be mentioned only as "a 2016 survey reported in the press."
- The same article cites KNBS "2020 index on women empowerment" (37% for unmarried women; reported to drop by 10 points on marriage or cohabitation). **Unverified** as stated. Check the KNBS source before use.
- The Nation article also reports the views of men on cost and freedom, not measured data.

**Not verified and not used:** headline claims such as "Kenyan men now running away from marriage" and "marriage pool has gotten shallower" appear as headlines or quotes only.

## B. What is being said (opinion, anecdote, framing)

Summaries are paraphrased. Attributed quotes are short and should be quoted only with names and roles given in the source.

### B1. Men opting out

- **Reasons heard from men (Nation, undated; interviews with named men aged 33–39):** marriage is an "avoidable, unnecessary expense"; men want to "get rich" first and value their freedom; fear a wife will marry for money; fear marriage because of a parent's divorce; say women now "want to be equal" and show less respect; see living with parents as an easy "free food and shelter." These are individual views. Do not treat them as a profile of Kenyan men.
- **Expert view (Nation):** Dr. Dennis Shilabukha, anthropologist, University of Nairobi (named in the article), says migration weakened the social pressure to marry, and that the "sponsor" phenomenon may delay marriage or lead to "revenge" marriages. Joseph Orinde, described as a sociologist with no affiliation given, says the marriage pool has become "shallower." Andrew Cherlin (US sociologist) calls marriage a "capstone" for men who are already settled.
- **Common online framing:** "red pill", MGTOW ("men going their own way"), "men are trash" and "women are the problem" discourse. These are slogans, often built on the worst examples on both sides. They are not data.

### B2. Women and reasons for avoiding marriage

- **Reasons heard from women (The Star, 2025):** a controlling boyfriend who expected her to "cook and clean for him like a wife" before any engagement; childhood sexual abuse; a polygamous upbringing where "submission was drilled into me"; a wish to choose a partner who fits her values, "emotionally, mentally, and financially." These are individual accounts. Several are about abuse, not about marriage as such.
- **Expert view (The Star):** psychologist Dr. Simon Sironka (named) says upbringing shapes attitudes and that "women stay in abusive marriages for the sake of their children." He cites no data. The article has no survey method.
- **Online framing:** "women are not ready to submit", "women want the benefits without accountability", "women are too independent." These claims are usually made about women in general and are rarely tied to evidence.

### B3. Feminism and equality

- **Common claims:** equal pay, safety, and respect for women's careers. Also: backlash against "toxic masculinity" language, and concern that gender-equality messages have become hostile to men.
- **Data on what each side wants:** the US MTF data (A2) shows the sharpest shift in young women's stated desire for marriage, which means the debate is often about women's *expectations* of marriage as well as men's.
- **Kenyan context:** the National Gender Equality Commission is quoted (The Star, 2023) saying legal protection for women in divorce or abandonment is important. The article links the lack of marriage registration to women losing property in a break-up.

### B4. Submission and headship

- **Popular teaching:** "wives must submit" is preached in some churches and rejected in others. In many Kenyan homes, "headship" is taken to mean the husband decides everything. Some women describe this as a source of fear.
- **Reaction:** many women say "submission" has been used to excuse control. Many men say they are blamed for everything. Both views are heard in the same families.
- **Perazim status:** the biblical texts (Eph 5:21–33; Col 3:18–19; 1 Pet 3:1–7) are the starting point. The *interpretation and application* is not settled as a Perazim position. Mark any headship answer as DRAFT for Levi.

### B5. Infidelity and the language used for it

- **Slang and sexual language:** Sheng uses many words for cheating and sex. Two forms that come up in member questions are *kugongewa* and *kugongeana*. Their exact sense (passive "to be cheated on / used" versus reciprocal "to cheat each other") and the connotation in each speech community **must be checked with a native speaker before the answer uses them.** Levi is the native speaker for this check. Do not guess.
- *Mpango wa kando* (a side arrangement, usually a second partner) is a common Swahili phrase. Confirm the connotation with Levi before use.
- **Common claims:** "everyone cheats"; "men cheat, women are forgiven"; "women cheat more now"; "sponsor culture is normal." These are opinions. The Nation article's 2016 survey claims are old and unverified (A3).

### B6. The "conspiracy against marriage" narrative

- **What is said:** that marriage is being deliberately undermined by media, feminism, government policy, foreign influence, or a small group that profits from divorce. Sometimes paired with religious warnings about "the last days."
- **What the evidence shows:** marriage patterns are changing across the world for many reasons: later marriage, cohabitation, education, careers, cost of living, migration, changing views of gender roles. No reviewed source shows coordination against marriage. Describe the trend. Do not confirm a plot.
- **Biblical handling:** marriage is under real pressure, and the enemy is real (Eph 6:12). The answer must not turn a cultural trend into a secret plot, and must not blame individual people or groups.

### B7. Economic pressure

- **Claims heard:** rising cost of living; high dowry or bride-price expectations; unemployment; the cost of a wedding; men feeling unable to provide and women feeling unable to rely on a partner; "sponsors" replacing dependable partners.
- **Data:** no reviewed source gives a reliable Kenyan figure on dowry or wedding costs. Do not invent one.
- **Dowry (approved):** acceptable when it honours both families; never a sale, a debt trap, or a reason to delay marriage.

### B8. Polygamy

- **Approved:** marriage is one man and one woman. Existing polygamous families receive pastoral care and are not told to abandon wives or children.
- **Data:** 9% of currently married women have a co-wife (KDHS 2022, as reported), down from 23% in 1989.

### B9. Divorce and remarriage — UNDECIDED

- **Heard:** many Kenyan women say they cannot leave an abusive marriage because they have no documents and no property rights; some men say they cannot divorce without losing everything. The Star reports that most women without a marriage certificate "walk out empty-handed."
- **Perazim status:** UNDECIDED. The answer must explain the passages (Matt 19:4–6; Mal 2:16; 1 Cor 7:10–11) and say the church has not settled the question. It must not decide whether a person may divorce or remarry.
- **Safeguarding is not undecided.** Any abuse, threat or danger goes to safety routes first (Section D).

## C. Patterns to keep in mind

1. **Both sides hurt.** Men describe being judged and cut off. Women describe control, abuse and being blamed. Answers that hear only one side fail both groups.
2. **Abuse changes the question.** Many "submission" and "accountability" arguments are really about abuse. Safety comes before doctrine.
3. **Individual stories are not statistics.** Use stories to show that people are hurting. Use data only with its source and its limits.
4. **Infidelity has a long shadow.** The betrayed spouse is often the one who asks for help. Give comfort and practical next steps, not verdicts on the cheater.
5. **Money matters.** Almost every complaint about marriage in Kenya includes money. Scripture has a lot to say about money, provision and fair dealing (Prov 31; Mal 2:16; Jas 5:4).
