## 1. Biblical passages and original context

- Isaiah 53:4–5: the Servant bore our griefs (*ḥolî*, *makʾōbîm*) and by his stripes we are healed.
- Matthew 8:16–17: Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healing ministry.
- 1 Peter 2:24: "by whose stripes ye were healed" (Greek *iáthēte*), set in the context of sin and righteousness.
- James 5:14–16: prayer of faith, anointing, and the forgiveness of sins with healing.
- 1 Corinthians 11:29–30: sickness and death among some in Corinth linked to the Lord's table.
- 2 Corinthians 12:7–10: Paul's "thorn" and the answer "my grace is sufficient".
- Philippians 2:25–27: Epaphroditus ill, then restored by God's mercy.
- 1 Timothy 5:23: Timothy's stomach and wine.
- 2 Timothy 4:20: Trophimus left sick at Miletus.
- Romans 8:18–23: creation groans; the redemption of the body.

## 2. Terms that affect interpretation

- Greek *iáomai* (heal, 1 Peter 2:24), *sṓzō* (save, and in the Gospels also heal), *sōtēría* (salvation, and in some contexts healing, wholeness).
- Hebrew *rāpāʾ* (heal) and *ḥālâ* (sick, to be weak).
- Whether 1 Peter 2:24 refers to physical healing, spiritual healing, or both, is the central interpretive question.
- Whether Matthew 8:17 applies Isaiah to physical healing or to the Servant's wider work is debated.

## 3. Wider biblical teaching

Scripture teaches that God heals, that suffering is real in a fallen world, that the body is redeemed in resurrection, and that God's grace sustains the faithful in weakness.

## 4. Historical Christian interpretations

Early and medieval Christians prayed for healing and also used medicine. Many writers saw healing as a gift, not a guarantee in this age. Charismatic and Pentecostal movements have emphasised healing in the atonement more strongly in the 20th century. Historical research is needed before this section is finalised.

## 5. Competing evangelical positions

1. Healing is provided in the atonement and is available now for every believer. Prayer should claim it, and sickness is a lack of faith or a sign of sin. *(Widely critiqued, including for its pastoral consequences.)*
2. Healing is secured by the atonement and is received in part now, and fully at the resurrection. Prayer for healing is right, and God may or may not heal in this age.
3. Physical healing is a gift of God, not a guaranteed right of the atonement. Some Reformed writers argue for a cessationist view of certain miraculous gifts.
4. Medicine and prayer are both good and do not contradict each other.

## 7. Pastoral implications and exceptions

- The sick must never be told that they lack faith because they are not healed.
- Medical care must not be discouraged. The Guide points to a qualified clinician for medical matters (G03 section 6).
- Grief after unanswered prayer is answered with compassion and Scripture (for example Psalm 13 and Lamentations), not blame.
- Members in acute danger or with symptoms of a medical emergency are directed to emergency services (922) first (G03 section 3).

## 8. What the AI may and may not say

**May:** explain the passages, terms and views fairly; encourage prayer and faith; direct a sick person to medical care and emergency services where needed.

**May not:** state Perazim's position; say a particular person will or will not be healed; blame a sick person for lack of faith or sin; discourage medical treatment; diagnose.
